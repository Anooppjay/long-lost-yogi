In the narrow passages of the Fes el-Bali medina, where the smell of the tannery reaches you three streets before the colour does, Hassan Al-Fassi has been working leather since he was nine years old.

His father's hands. His grandfather's tools. The same vat of saffron and pomegranate dye that has been used in this family for four generations.

Nothing here was mass manufactured. No factory, no bulk order, no middleman. Every bag leaves Hassan's workshop having been touched by the same pair of hands from the first cut of hide to the last stitch of the gusset.

We found him through a network of weavers and craftspeople we had built over three years of travelling the medinas of North Africa. A contact in Essaouira mentioned a leather worker in Fes who still did it the old way. It took us six weeks to find him.

When we arrived, he was working in a room barely large enough to turn around in, the air thick with the smell of natural tannins. On the wall behind him, three bags were drying on wooden pegs. Each one took him four days.

He did not understand why anyone in Australia would want to buy something he had made. He had never sold outside of Morocco. His work had never needed to travel further than the souk at the end of his street.

You are the last chapter of a story that started in someone's hands. When you carry this bag, you carry the weight of a craft that has survived empire, revolution, modernisation and the mass market — not because it was protected, but because Hassan never stopped making it.

The leather is full-grain, sourced from the same cooperative that supplies the traditional Chouara tannery. The dye is natural. The stitching is hand-waxed linen thread. There are no shortcuts in this bag because there are no shortcuts in Hassan's life.
